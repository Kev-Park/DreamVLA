Humanoid Vision-Language-Action Policies

Abstract: Training vision-language-action (VLA) policies typically depends on
1
substantial robot demonstration data, making human teleoperation a costly and
2
cumbersome bottleneck, particularly for whole-body humanoid interaction. Re-
3
inforcement learning in simulation offers an alternative for scaling this data, but
4
designing task-specific rewards remains challenging. To address this, we pro-
5
pose DreamVLA, which uses generated motion trajectories to guide reinforcement
6
learning (RL) in simulation, converting them into reliable task execution trajecto-
7
ries. These trajectories train VLA models in simulation without new task-specific
8
teleoperation data. Our central contribution is a reference-conditioned DAgger
9
distillation procedure: after a failed learner rollout, we restart the RL expert from
10
a sampled learner state with the same reference suffix and aggregate successful
11
corrective trajectories, addressing the distribution shift left by offline imitation.
12
We evaluate picking from a platform, opening a drawer, and pressing a button,
13
with ablations of DAgger iterations, residual RL, and generated dataset scale.
14
Keywords: Humanoid manipulation, synthetic demonstrations, VLA distillation

Introduction
16
Vision-language-action (VLA) models offer a common interface for mapping visual observations
17
and natural-language instructions to robot behavior. Models such as OpenVLA and GR00T learn
18
from robot demonstrations and can be adapted to downstream tasks through additional training [1, 2].
19
For a humanoid, these demonstrations must coordinate reaching, hand interaction, torso motion, foot
20
placement, and balance. A hierarchical interface can use a low-level (system-1) controller such as
21
SONIC [7] to execute robot motion tokens supplied by a higher-level policy or a human teleoperation
22
interface. Using this interface, an operator collects interactive demonstrations for VLA training.
23
Collecting this behavior repeatedly across scene configurations demands operator time, a suitable
24
teleoperation interface, and careful supervision. Increasing the number of tasks and environmental
25
variations therefore increases the human effort required to build useful training data.
26
Simulation offers a way to scale this data, but replacing a teleoperator with an autonomous data
27
generator introduces a control problem. Reinforcement learning in simulation with sparse task suc-
28
cess gives a high-dimensional humanoid little guidance for approaching a target while maintaining
29
balance [3]. Dense rewards can support exploration, yet often require task-specific choices about
30
reaching, posture, contact, timing, and object progress [9, 12]. A policy can also satisfy parts of a
31
reward while producing behavior that is poorly suited to imitation. Consequently, the usefulness of
32
simulated demonstrations depends on both physical task completion and the quality of the behavior
33
used to achieve it.
34
Generative human motion models provide a complementary source of structure. Text and spatial
35
constraints can specify the intended behavior and the location of an interaction, yielding whole-
36
body motion references with a human motion prior. DreamControl combines such references with

We propose DreamVLA, a framework for producing task demonstrations in simulation from gener-
43
ated human motions and distilling them into a VLA. We first generate task-conditioned references
44
with DreamControl, using OmniControl to constrain relevant human joints at target locations and
45
times [4]. After retargeting these motions to the Unitree G1, we train an expert to co-track the hu-
46
manoid and the interacting object. We use SONIC as the whole-body control backbone [7], and
47
learn residual corrections in its motion-token interface to adapt the reference behavior to contact
48
and randomized physics. The resulting expert acts as an automated demonstration generator: mo-
49
tion synthesis supplies the intended human-like behavior, and RL supplies the physically executed
50
interaction. Successful rollouts are recorded as a LeRobot dataset and used to fine-tune GR00T.
51
This construction is analogous to collecting teleoperation data with a generative model supplying
52
the motion commands. It removes the need to have a human operate the robot for every downstream
53
demonstration while retaining an interface suited to whole-body control. The human motion prior
54
and the pretrained control and VLA backbones remain valuable upstream resources. Our claim
55
concerns eliminating new task-specific teleoperation collection for the simulated tasks, rather than
56
eliminating human data from the pretraining of those resources. We formulate each interactive task
57
using motion references, object behavior, and a success specification; the present study evaluates
58
three tasks.
59
The central research question is how to distill this expert into a VLA that remains competent under
60
its own closed-loop execution. Successful expert trajectories are a useful starting point, but they
61
emphasize states reached by the expert. A student that grasps slightly late, approaches a handle at
62
a different angle, or misaligns its fingertip can visit states absent from that dataset. Its next action
63
changes the next observation, allowing small errors to compound. Collecting more clean demonstra-
64
tions may improve coverage of nominal behavior without providing the corrective behavior needed
65

after these deviations. Offline imitation alone therefore leaves a gap between supervised action
66
prediction and reliable interaction.
67
We address this gap through a reference-conditioned DAgger distillation procedure that collects
68
corrective trajectories after learner failures. Standard DAgger aggregates expert labels on states
69
encountered by the learner [8], and prior humanoid teacher–student systems such as VIRAL already
70
use online DAgger [9]. Our task-specific adaptation queries a generated-motion, residual-SONIC
71
expert at a randomly sampled state of a failed learner rollout. The expert resumes with the suffix of
72
the same generated reference, and only successful recovery trajectories are added to the aggregate.
73
This supplies temporally consistent corrective demonstrations for the language-conditioned VLA
74
while retaining the reference context required by the expert.
75
The evaluation is organized around picking from a platform, opening a drawer, and pressing a button
76
in simulation. These tasks expose distinct interaction requirements: establishing and maintaining a
77
grasp, driving an articulated object, and reaching a precise contact target. We present results from
78
controlled studies of the number of DAgger iterations, the residual control formulation, and the
79
amount of generated training data. Together, task completion, expert data yield, motion fidelity, and
80
the student–expert performance gap characterize demonstration quality and the competence retained
81
through distillation.
82
Our three contributions are:
83
1. A generative demonstration framework. We propose a pipeline that uses generated hu-
84
man motions and residual RL on a SONIC backbone to collect natural, human-like inter-
85
action demonstrations for VLA training in simulation, without task-specific human teleop-
86
eration collection.
87
2. A DAgger-based VLA distillation method. We propose using the RL expert to supervise
88
the VLA on learner-visited states, with reference-conditioned expert recovery and success-
89
filtered trajectory aggregation as the methodological contribution.
90
3. A three-task simulation study with controlled ablations. We evaluate platform pick-
91
ing, drawer opening, and button pressing with controlled ablations of DAgger iterations,
92
residual RL, and generated data scale.

Related Work
94
2.1
Humanoid Motion Tracking and Teleoperation
95
Human-motion priors support humanoid tracking and whole-body control [13–19], while cam-
96
era, VR, and portable interfaces support teleoperation and demonstration collection [20–25]. We
97
use SONIC’s motion-token interface [7] to turn generated references into autonomous interaction
98
demonstrations. Generated motions supply the nominal commands in our pipeline, and task-specific
99
residual RL adjusts their execution at contact without collecting new teleoperated demonstrations.
100
2.2
Vision-Language-Action Learning for Humanoids
101
Humanoid VLAs connect visual-language representations to executable behavior through mul-
102
timodal pretraining and reusable motor controllers [2, 26–30].
Synthetic kinematic and
103
optimal-control demonstrations also provide supervision for whole-body visual policies [31, 32].
104
DreamVLA uses a generated-motion, residual-SONIC expert to produce physically executed inter-
105
actions and reference-conditioned corrective trajectories for GR00T. We fine-tune an existing VLA
106
backbone and study demonstration generation and corrective supervision, without introducing a new
107
model architecture.

2.3
Visual Sim-to-Real Across Embodiments
109
Across manipulation, legged, and aerial embodiments, visual sim-to-real combines simulated expe-
110
rience with perception randomization, privileged supervision, or reconstructed scenes [33–46]. Hu-
111
manoid pipelines use teacher–student distillation [9, 12]. We study VLA distillation in simulation.
112
Our pipeline shares privileged-to-visual supervision with these humanoid systems, while isolating
113
motion-guided expert training and corrective VLA distillation without visual domain randomization.
114
2.4
Motion Generation, Retargeting, and Synthetic Demonstrations
115
Language-conditioned motion models provide interaction references [4, 47–51], while retargeting
116
and demonstration augmentation adapt motions to robot morphology and new scenes [5, 6, 52–54].
117
Generative video and simulated experts offer complementary routes to synthetic policy data [55–
118
59]. We build on DreamControl [3] to produce physically executed SONIC demonstrations from
119
guided human motion. Residual RL resolves the contact dynamics left by kinematic synthesis, and
120
successful-rollout filtering converts the resulting interactions into executable action labels for VLA
121
training.
122
2.5
Expert Distillation and Corrective Data Aggregation
123
DAgger-style distillation addresses learner-induced distribution shift [8, 9, 60, 61]. Our adaptation
124
restores a state from a failed VLA rollout, resumes the expert with the same reference suffix, and
125
aggregates only successful corrective trajectories for GR00T action-chunk training. This retains the
126
DAgger principle of querying learner-visited states while collecting full, phase-consistent recovery
127
segments for action-chunk training.

Methodology
129
DreamVLA comprises four stages: reference generation, residual expert training and successful
130
rollout collection, GR00T distillation with iterative expert recovery, and simulation validation (Fig-
131
ure 1). Let ℓdenote a task instruction, st the full simulator state, and ot = (It, pt) the student
132
observation, consisting of camera images It and robot proprioception pt. The expert uses privileged
133
simulator information; the VLA predicts executable commands from ot and ℓ. Privileged object
134
states used for expert training and success checks are excluded from the student observation.
135

Why iterative supervision is needed.
The initialized student is trained on expert-generated ob-
208
servations and then evaluated on observations induced by its own actions. These distributions differ
209
whenever perception, timing, or action prediction changes the interaction. This matters particularly
210
at contact: a small error can alter whether an object is grasped, whether a drawer handle is engaged,
211
or whether a button is actuated. The appropriate next command may then differ substantially from
212
the next command in the original successful trajectory. Iterative distillation exposes the VLA to such
213
states and obtains expert supervision appropriate to the current interaction.
214

4.2
Effectiveness of Residual RL on SONIC
261
This study isolates the choice to learn residual SONIC token commands. We compare both the RL
262
and downstream GR00T performance at k = 3. We train RL for the same budget of 3000 training
263
iterations. As can be observed from the performance, using residual RL significantly reduces the
264
amount of iterations needed for RL to accomplish the task in simulation. We also report perfor-
265
mance of using SONIC commands as-is with no residual RL. Without residual adaptation, nominal
266
SONIC tracking has a task success rate close to zero. This is because the base SONIC controller has
267
some tracking error which makes it hard for deploying zero-shot on precise tasks, also kinematic
268
references may not be exactly trackable. Residual RL makes small trajectory corrections needed
269
to complete the task while retaining the nominal SONIC motion, and compensates for the forces
270
needed to pick an object or open a heavy drawer. Figure 3 compares matched video frames from
271
nominal and residual execution. In this rollout, nominal SONIC tips the mustard bottle off the
272
counter, whereas residual control retains the bottle in the hand.

Limitations
280
The evaluation is limited to three simulated interaction tasks on a single humanoid embodi-
281
ment. Generated motions can be incompatible with robot kinematics or contact requirements, and
282
task-specific guidance, filtering, object references, and reward weights remain necessary parts of
283
the pipeline. Successful-rollout filtering may underrepresent difficult configurations. Reference-
284
conditioned experts may provide unreliable or abrupt corrections on learner-visited states, particu-
285
larly during precise interactions.
286
6
Conclusion and Future Work
287
We present a framework for turning generated human motion into simulated humanoid demonstra-
288
tions and using them to train a VLA. DreamControl supplies task-conditioned references, residual
289
RL on a SONIC backbone adapts them to robot–object interaction, and successful rollouts initialize
290
GR00T fine-tuning. Reference-conditioned recovery DAgger distills expert behavior under learner-
291
induced state distributions by aggregating successful corrective continuations.
292
Future work focuses on expanding the task set and the diversity of interacting objects, studying
293
longer task sequences, and quantifying data-generation efficiency at larger scale. A further direction
294
is sim-to-real deployment, informed by the visual humanoid transfer pipelines of DoorMan and
295
VIRAL [9, 12]. Key requirements include validating the control and sensing stack on hardware,
296
matching cameras and contact behavior, and studying visual and physical randomization. These
297
directions extend the present simulation study; hardware deployment is outside its scope.
